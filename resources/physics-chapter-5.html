Work, Energy and Power

Topic-by-topic explanations

1. Introduction

Work, energy and power provide scalar methods for analysing motion. Work is energy transferred by a force through displacement.

2. Notions of work and kinetic energy: The work-energy theorem

For a constant force W=Fs cosθ, where θ is the angle between force and displacement. Work is positive along displacement, negative opposite it and zero when perpendicular.

3. Work

Kinetic energy is K=½mv². The work-energy theorem states that the net work by all forces equals ΔK=K_f−K_i.

4. Kinetic energy

For a variable force, work is the signed area under the force-displacement graph, or W=∫F_x dx. Segments where force opposes displacement contribute negative work.

5. Work done by a variable force

The work-energy theorem remains valid for variable forces: total work from all forces equals the change in kinetic energy. It avoids finding acceleration or time when work and speed are known.

6. The work-energy theorem for a variable force

Potential energy is associated with configuration for a conservative force. Near Earth's surface U_g=mgh; for an ideal spring U_s=½kx². Only changes in the chosen zero level matter.

7. The concept of potential energy

When only conservative forces do work, K+U remains constant. With non-conservative work, Δ(K+U)=W_non-conservative; friction often transfers mechanical energy to thermal energy.

8. The conservation of mechanical energy

A spring stretched or compressed by x stores U=½kx², valid in the linear Hooke-law range. Doubling the extension quadruples the stored energy.

9. The potential energy of a spring

Average power is W/Δt and instantaneous power is P=F·v. Watt is joule per second. Efficiency is useful output divided by input, multiplied by 100%.

10. Power

In an isolated collision momentum is conserved. An elastic collision also conserves total kinetic energy; inelastic collisions do not, and in a perfectly inelastic collision the bodies stick together.

Worked example

A 2 kg cart speeds up from 3 to 7 m s⁻¹. Net work is ½m(v²−u²)=40 J. If this takes 5 s, average net power is 8 W.

Common errors and revision checklist
