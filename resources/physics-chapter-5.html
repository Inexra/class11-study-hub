Work, Energy and Power

Work measures energy transferred by a force through displacement. Conservation laws and energy methods link speed, height, power and collisions.

Practice Question Paper · 40 marks ↗Full syllabus index

Complete revision notes

1. Introduction

For constant force W=Fs cosθ. Work is positive along displacement, negative opposite it and zero for a perpendicular force.

2. Notions of work and kinetic energy: The work-energy theorem

Kinetic energy K=½mv²; net work W_net=ΔK. Variable-force work is signed area under F-x or ∫F dx.

3. Work

Conservative forces have path-independent work and potential energy. Near Earth U=mgh; spring potential energy is ½kx²; W_cons=−ΔU.

4. Kinetic energy

Mechanical energy K+U is conserved when non-conservative work is zero. Otherwise Δ(K+U)=W_non-cons; friction may turn mechanical energy into thermal energy.

5. Work done by a variable force

Power is rate of energy transfer: P_avg=W/t and instantaneous P=F·v. Efficiency=useful output/input×100%.

6. The work-energy theorem for a variable force

Isolated collisions conserve momentum. Elastic collisions also conserve kinetic energy; in perfectly inelastic collisions bodies stick together.

7. The concept of potential energy

Isolated collisions conserve momentum. Elastic collisions also conserve kinetic energy; in perfectly inelastic collisions bodies stick together.

8. The conservation of mechanical energy

Isolated collisions conserve momentum. Elastic collisions also conserve kinetic energy; in perfectly inelastic collisions bodies stick together.

9. The potential energy of a spring

10. Power

11. Collisions

Formula sheet

Worked example

A 2 kg cart speeds from 3 to 7 m/s: net work=40 J. If the change takes 5 s, average net power=8 W.

Common errors and revision checklist

Use net work from all forces; momentum conservation does not imply kinetic-energy conservation; preserve the sign of work.
